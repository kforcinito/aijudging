Work stream 2

Overview

The AI will score routines from one division at the 2027 Youth Summit, but not live at the event due to infrastructure constraints. Instead, after the event, the AI scores 30–50 recorded routines from that division, and we compare its scores to the panel's actual scores. This tells us whether the AI is accurate, consistent from run to run, and catching anything judges miss. To start, we need the division's rubric and a confirmed video source.

POC scope

Validation only (“shadow mode”)

  • No live AI scoring this season. The AI never becomes the system of record, and judges stay the decision-makers.
  • The POC scores recorded routines after an event: about 30–50 sessions from one division, using a variety of routines by quality (below average, average, and above average). The AI's scores are compared against the actual judges' scores.
  • We're measuring three things: whether scores are consistent from run to run, what the AI catches that judges miss (and the reverse), and whether AI can do this job at all.

Why not live

Unwritten criteria

Subjective scoring criteria aren't written down, for example what separates an 8 from a 9.

Inconsistent focus

Early tests show the AI is inconsistent about what it focuses on.

Limited training data

We don't have enough training data yet.

Venue bandwidth

Many venues only have about 3 Mbps of bandwidth.

Initial scope

First

All Star Cheer

All Star Cheer comes first because its rubric is more objective: a given skill earns a given number of points. School and dance come later.

First

Objective and difficulty scoring

The objective and difficulty side comes first. The subjective and artistic side is a stretch goal, and the business will decide whether to pursue it.

Youth Summit target (Apr/May 2027)

The “live” checkpoint: if the model is ready, we can decide whether to have it score in parallel at a table next to the panel. That's close to real time but not live, and it's for comparison only. We don't have to wait until April: we can start scoring historical video as far back as we have it.

How it works

Score

Recorded routines

After the event, the AI scores 30–50 recorded routines from one division using that division's rubric.

Compare

Agreement analysis

AI scores are lined up against the panel's actual scores by category to measure agreement and surface routines where the two diverge most.

Review

Consistency and misses

Routines are rescored to check the AI gives the same result from run to run, and the largest gaps are reviewed to see whether the AI caught anything judges missed.

After the event only: AI scores are produced after results are final and have no effect on results, rankings, or awards. They exist only to evaluate the technology.

What success looks like

  • Agreement with the panel's actual scores within an accepted tolerance across rubric categories.
  • Consistent scores when the same routine is scored more than once.
  • Evidence of whether the AI catches anything judges miss.
  • Clear explanations for each AI score that a senior judge can verify.
  • A body of evidence strong enough to support a confident decision to scale the initiative.

What needs to be true for live AI scoring

Before AI can score live at events, four things need to be in place.

Improved event infrastructure

Improved infrastructure at events, with an on-site setup that can capture and process video while the competition is running.

Objective definitions

Written, objective definitions for skills and scoring rules.

Judge baseline

A baseline for how accurate our judges are today.

Proven consistency

Proof that the AI scores consistently and within an agreed tolerance of the judges.

Open items

Questions the AI Scoring team needs to resolve before or during the POC.

  • Bandwidth at venues: Michael is following up with James and Kelly Green. Live streaming runs at 5–15 Mbps on a separate network from SimpleScore.
  • Division and rubric: which division and rubric the POC will use.
  • Video source: a confirmed source for the 30–50 recorded routines.
  • Video rights and consent: Varsity TV vs. Flo, and how routine video is consented, stored, and retained.
  • Accuracy parity: how we define it, including sample size and tolerance, per rubric category.
  • CapEx and budget.
