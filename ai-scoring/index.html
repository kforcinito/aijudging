Overview

Phase 1 is a proof of concept for April 2027. The AI watches Level 4 All Star routine video and gives judges counts (athletes, tosses, jumps), flags (out of bounds, over time), and section tags to review. It does not score anything and issues no deductions. Judges keep scoring as they do today and use the AI output as an extra check. Each item links to its moment in the video, so a judge can verify it in seconds. The AI's counts and flags are graded against an answer key from a rules expert, which tells us whether the AI is accurate and fast enough to be useful to judges.

POC scope

Validation only (“shadow mode”)

  • No AI scoring this season. The AI counts, flags, and tags; it never issues a score or deduction, never becomes the system of record, and judges stay the decision-makers.
  • The POC uses about 15 Level 4 routines across age groups, with final score sheets: 5 strong (no deductions), 5 average (1–2 deductions), and 5 weak (3+ deductions). The AI's counts and flags are graded against a rules expert's answer key, with the judge panel's results as a second check.
  • We're measuring three things: whether the AI counts and flags accurately, whether results are ready within 60 seconds of the routine ending, and whether judges find the output useful.

Why the AI doesn't score yet

Unwritten criteria

Subjective scoring criteria aren't written down, for example what separates an 8 from a 9.

Inconsistent focus

Early tests show the AI is inconsistent about what it focuses on.

Limited training data

We don't have enough training data yet.

Venue bandwidth

Many venues only have about 3 Mbps of bandwidth, which will help decide whether judges can use the output live.

Initial scope

First

All Star Level 4

All Star Cheer comes first because its rubric is more objective: a given skill earns a given number of points. Phase 1 covers Level 4 only, with a 2:30 routine length. Other levels and school divisions come later.

First

Counts and flags, not scores

Athlete count, tosses, jumps, out-of-bounds and overtime flags, and section tags come first, with tumbling as a stretch goal. Execution quality, stunts, pyramids, and deductions are out of scope for Phase 1.

Youth Summit target (Apr/May 2027)

Phase 1 requirements

Phase 1 is a proof of concept (POC) for April 2027. The AI watches Level 4 All Star routine video and gives judges counts, flags, and section tags to review. It does not score anything. Judges keep scoring as they do today and use the AI output as an extra check. Each item links to its moment in the video, so a judge can verify it in seconds.

Why: the long-term goal is faster, more consistent judging and a path to the 2028 savings target ($100K). Savings may come from shorter event days and more efficient judging, plus new value for customers, not only from smaller judging panels.

What's included

Phase 1 covers All Star Level 4 routines only, with a 2:30 routine length.

CategoryItems
In scopeTotal athlete count, tosses (type and count), jumps (type, count, connected), out-of-bounds flag, overtime flag, and section flags and tags across the whole routine
Stretch goalTumbling counts and skill types: standing tumbling first, then running tumbling. Attempted if feasible; not required for POC success.

Output

Requested target: AI output is ready within 60 seconds of the routine ending, ideally 10 to 30 seconds. Feasibility is still to be determined.

Count

A number plus skill types

Each with a timestamp: total athletes on the floor, tosses by type, and jumps by type and whether they're connected.

Flag

A note for the judge

No deduction is issued. Out of bounds, and over time (2-second “warning” or 3+ second “deduction”).

Tag

Where each section starts and ends

Building, standing tumbling, running tumbling, jumps, and tosses.

  • Out of bounds: both feet fully off the mat, including the border. Shows the timestamp and “2 feet out of bounds.”
  • Overtime: two flag levels. Level 1 is over 2:30 and flags a “warning.” Level 2 is over 2:33 (the deduction threshold), flags a “deduction,” and shows how far over.
  • Tags can overlap: a stunt during running tumbling gets a building tag inside the running tumbling section. Judges should be able to jump to every place a section type appears, since running tumbling can happen many times in one routine.
  • Settings, not hard-coded: routine length is a setting (2:30 for the POC) and could later come from ERMP based on level and division. Level and division are settings too, so later phases can add levels without a rebuild.

Skill rules

The AI only needs to label each skill and count it. It doesn't judge how well the skill was done. Skills are built in this order, easiest to hardest:

  1. Athlete count: the number of athletes on the floor when the routine starts. This drives every quantity rule below.
  2. Tosses: type and count. A majority of the team must perform a Level 4 toss in the same section (a single portion of the routine where skills from that skill set are performed). Required numbers come from the building quantity chart. Level 4 tosses: ball kick, pike X, pike split, hitch kick, switch kick, double toe touch, ball full, pike full, kick full, toe touch full, full up toe touch, double full.
  3. Jumps: type, count, and whether connected. Only scorable advanced jumps count; choreography jumps are ignored. Level 4 jumps: toe touch, pike, front hurdler (right or left), side hurdler (right or left), double nine.
    • Connected means back-to-back, using a whip approach (continuous arm swing between jumps).
    • Variety means at least 2 different jumps. A left and a right hurdler count as the same jump.
    • Jumps only count if the athlete lands on their feet. Required numbers come from the tumbling/jump quantity chart.
  4. Stretch goal 1, standing tumbling: type and count, using the 10 standing passes on the L4 Tumbling List.
  5. Stretch goal 2, running tumbling: type and count, using the running passes on the L4 Tumbling List. Many passes include a “transition” (any skills between the first and last skill), which makes them harder to identify. For both tumbling types, the athlete must land on their feet; a jump inside a pass doesn't break it up, but a T-jump does.

How we compare: for each routine, a rules expert reviews the same video and records the correct counts and flags (the “answer key”). AI results are graded against that answer key. Comparing the AI to the judge panel's results is a second check.

Business case: the POC doesn't save money on its own. It shows whether the AI is accurate and fast enough to support the 2028 savings target ($100K) through faster judging, shorter event days and, later, smaller panels or new customer features that can be monetized.

How it works

Watch

Count, flag, and tag

The AI watches the routine video and returns counts, flags, and section tags, each with a timestamp, ideally within 10 to 30 seconds of the routine ending.

Verify

Judges check in seconds

Each item links to its moment in the video, so a judge can jump straight to it and confirm or ignore it.

Grade

Answer key

A rules expert reviews the same video and records the correct counts and flags. AI results are graded against that answer key, and compared with the judge panel's results as a second check.

No effect on results: the AI issues no scores or deductions. Its output has no effect on results, rankings, or awards, and exists only to support judges and evaluate the technology.

What success looks like

  • Counts and flags meet the proposed accuracy targets: athlete count exact in 95% of routines, 90% match on tosses and jumps, 90% of out-of-bounds caught with under 10% false flags, and 100% of overtime caught.
  • Results are ready within 60 seconds of the routine ending.
  • Most POC judges rate the tags, counts, and flags useful.
  • Every item links to its moment in the video, so a judge can verify it.
  • Evidence strong enough to decide whether AI can support the 2028 savings target ($100K) and later phases.

What needs to be true for live AI scoring

Before AI can score live at events, four things need to be in place.

Improved event infrastructure

Improved infrastructure at events, with an on-site setup that can capture and process video while the competition is running.

Objective definitions

Written, objective definitions for skills and scoring rules.

Judge baseline

A baseline for how accurate our judges are today.

Proven consistency

Proof that the AI scores consistently and within an agreed tolerance of the judges.

Open items

Questions the AI Scoring team needs to resolve before or during the POC.

  • Bandwidth at venues: Michael is following up with James and Kelly Green. Live streaming runs at 5–15 Mbps on a separate network from SimpleScore.
  • Live vs. after the fact: whether judges use the output live (Option A) or it runs in the background and is compared afterward (Option B), based on processing speed and venue bandwidth.

  • Video source: a confirmed source for the ~15 Level 4 routines and their final score sheets.
  • Video rights and consent: Varsity TV vs. Flo, and how routine video is consented, stored, and retained.
  • Success targets: confirming the proposed accuracy, speed, and usefulness targets.
  • CapEx and budget.
