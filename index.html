One initiative, two work streams

The AI Judging project explores how AI can make competition scoring more consistent, more transparent, and less demanding on judging panels, without taking the final call away from trained human judges. The work is split into two complementary streams so each can be scoped, tested, and evaluated independently. Each stream has its own page with the full plan, status, and open questions.

Work stream 1

Eyes Up Judging

Keeps judges' eyes on the performance, not on the score sheet. Judges currently spend part of every routine looking down to write, tally, or enter scores. Eyes Up moves that busywork to an AI assistant that captures, organizes, and totals what the judge observes in real time.

  • Hands-free capture of deductions, skills, and notes
  • A live score sheet mapped to the official rubric
  • Judge review and sign-off before anything is submitted

Role of AI: assistant only. Every submitted score belongs to the judge.

Work stream 2

AI Scoring

An AI judge rides along with the human panel, scoring the same routines in shadow mode. Its results are recorded privately and compared with official scores after each event to measure accuracy, spot bias, and build the evidence needed before any wider use.

  • Shadow scoring against the same rubric as the panel
  • Post-event agreement analysis by rubric category
  • Senior-judge review of the largest gaps

AI Scoring MVP: ready Friday, April 16, 2027. See the full timeline on the stream page.

How the streams fit together: Eyes Up improves the judging experience today, and AI Scoring builds the evidence needed to decide what role AI scoring can responsibly play tomorrow. Both use the same scoring rubric as the human panel.

Guardrails

These principles apply to both work streams and to any future use of AI in judging.

Humans decide

Trained judges remain the final authority on every official score. AI supports and informs; it does not overrule.

One rubric

AI tools work from the same official scoring rubric and rules as the panel, so results are directly comparable.

Explainable

Every AI-generated score or suggestion must be traceable to specific moments in the routine and specific rubric criteria.

Athlete privacy

Video and performance data are used only for approved project purposes, stored securely, and handled with extra care for minors.

AI assists the panel. The panel owns the score.

Shared decisions

Questions that affect both work streams. Stream-specific questions live on each stream's page.

  • Pilot events: which competitions, divisions, and levels are included in the first pilots.
  • Communication: what judges, coaches, and event staff are told about each pilot, and when.

Future direction

The first phase is about learning safely. It does not attempt to replace judging panels or change official results.

Judge training

Use AI Scoring comparisons and Eyes Up records to give judges personal calibration feedback and to build training material from real routines.

Score reviews

Use timestamped Eyes Up notes and AI explanations to make score inquiries faster and easier to resolve, with evidence tied to the video.

Coach and athlete feedback

Over time, turn structured judging data into clearer post-event feedback that helps teams understand where points were earned and lost.

Earned trust: Any expanded role for AI scoring depends on evidence from AI Scoring, judge buy-in, and an explicit decision by the project leadership.
